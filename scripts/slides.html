The problem

Paper logs, wet gloves, one misheard digit

  • Every US food establishment is required to log temperatures — most of it is still clipboards, checked with wet, gloved hands, hours after the fact.
  • A 38°F reading logged as 48°F isn't a UX nitpick — it's a real food-safety failure, and it's invisible until an inspector (or an outbreak) finds it.
  • The fix has to be hands-free (cooks are working) and has to catch the misheard digit in the same breath, not at the next audit.

The idea

Say it once. Hear it read back. It's logged.

  • A cook calls out a reading mid-task — "walk-in cooler, thirty-eight," "chicken breast, one fifty-two."
  • TempCheck logs it, checks it against real FDA limits, and speaks the exact number back before saving anything flagged.
  • Out-of-range readings get a spoken corrective action, not a silent red dot nobody sees until the shift ends.

Why the rule engine is separate from the LLM

The safety verdict is never a language model's guess

  • The model's only job is extraction: location/item, number, unit. A plain, unit-tested comparison against FDA Food Code limits decides safe / amber / red.
  • Cold holding ≤41°F · hot holding ≥135°F · poultry ≥165°F · ground meat ≥155°F · whole muscle / fish / eggs ≥145°F.
  • 25 automated tests cover every category, every boundary, unit conversion, and implausible readings — zero hallucination risk in the verdict itself.

Business value

Every kitchen already has to do this

  • Temperature logging isn't optional — it's a health-code requirement at every US food establishment.
  • TempCheck turns a paper chore into a real-time, hands-free, provably-checked habit, with the exported PDF already in the format an inspector expects.
  • No new hardware: it runs in a browser tab a kitchen already has open.

Originality

One of ~280 submissions. None target food safety.

Most submissions in this hackathon build general-purpose copilots, dispatch/fraud agents, or interview coaches. TempCheck is deliberately narrow: one real workflow, done with enough rigor — deterministic rules, mandatory readback, measured accuracy, real end-to-end verification — that it could plausibly go into a real kitchen tomorrow.

What's next

Known limitations, stated plainly

  • Cooling-curve time tracking (135°F→70°F within 2h, then →41°F within 4h) — only point-in-time readings are checked today.
  • No persistent multi-shift storage yet — a shift's log lives in the browser tab and exports to PDF before ending.
  • Phone/SIP deployment for kitchens that don't want a browser tab open.
